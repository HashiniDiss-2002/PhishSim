{{ icon('send', size=14) }} Getting started, step by step
  1. Upload your employee roster. Go to Upload Employees and submit a CSV with columns full_name, email, department, job_title. Re-uploading replaces the existing roster for your organization (or your own department, if you're a department admin).
  2. Create a campaign. Go to New Campaign, give it a name, choose a condition (generic or personalized pretexts), a delivery vector (email link or callback/TOAD), and optionally a Stage 2 real-world reference scenario.
  3. Send a test email to yourself first. On the campaign's results page, use Send Test Email before launching — it goes only to your own admin account, with a clearly labelled placeholder link rather than a working tracking link.
  4. Check your email security settings. See "Why aren't simulation emails reaching employee inboxes?" below before you launch — this is the single most common setup issue.
  5. Launch the campaign. Use Send Campaign to deliver it to every employee in scope. Results, compliance findings, and the risk dashboard populate as employees interact with it.
  6. Review the dashboard. Your organization overview shows click/submission/report rates, compliance findings with exact GDPR/PDPA citations, and the top 5 riskiest and top 5 quickest-responding employees.

This is the most common setup issue, and it's expected behaviour, not a bug: PhishSim's simulation emails are deliberately designed to look like phishing (that's the point), so your organization's own email security (Microsoft 365 Defender, Google Workspace, Proofpoint, Mimecast, etc.) will often quarantine or block them exactly as it should for a real attack.

There are two different things people mean by "the sending domain" here — don't confuse them:

  • The spoofed "From" address shown to employees (e.g. "Acme IT Support <no-reply@acme-c0rp-portal.com>") — this is a synthetic look-alike identity (see services/domain_spoofing.py), generated purely for realism. It is never the real infrastructure the email is actually sent from.
  • The real sending infrastructure — whatever SMTP relay this PhishSim instance is configured with (e.g. a Mailtrap sandbox, or your organization's own relay via the SMTP_HOST environment variable). This is the one your email gateway actually needs to allow.

Step by step:

  1. Ask whoever configured this PhishSim deployment for the real outbound sending domain/IP (the SMTP_HOST/SMTP_FROM_ADDRESS it's using) — not the spoofed display name shown in a campaign.
  2. In your email security console, add a narrow, time-boxed allow rule for that specific sending domain/IP only — not a blanket rule for the spoofed domain, and not a permanent one:
    • Microsoft 365 Defender: Email & collaboration → Policies & rules → Threat policies → Tenant Allow/Block Lists → add the sending domain/IP as a temporary allow entry, or create a scoped Exchange mail flow rule that bypasses spam filtering for mail from that sender during your simulation window.
    • Google Workspace: Admin console → Apps → Google Workspace → Gmail → Spam, Phishing and Malware → add an approved sender list entry scoped to the sending domain/IP, or a dedicated inbound gateway rule for the simulation window.
    • Other gateways (Proofpoint, Mimecast, Barracuda, etc.): look for a "bypass filtering for sender" or temporary allow-list feature scoped to a specific sender, not a global domain exception.
  3. Remove the allow rule again once the simulation window ends. Leaving a standing exception in place weakens your real phishing defences for longer than necessary — scope it to the campaign, not permanently.
  4. Re-send a test email to yourself (step 3 of the setup guide above) to confirm it now lands.

Never allow-list the spoofed display domain itself in a way that would also let a real attacker using the same look-alike domain through — the allow rule should key off the actual sending infrastructure, scoped as narrowly as your email security product allows.

Four columns, in any order, with a header row: full_name, email, department, job_title. A department admin's upload is automatically forced into their own department regardless of what the CSV says — this isn't an error, it's the department-scoping rule. Re-uploading replaces the existing roster rather than appending to it.

MFA (an authenticator-app code, via TOTP) is mandatory for every admin/engineer account and has no in-app opt-out. If someone has lost the device their authenticator app was on, there's no self-service recovery in this MVP — a core admin should reset their password (Manage Admins → Reset Password), which emails a reset link; MFA on that account can then be re-enrolled from scratch the next time they log in.

A core admin (no department set on their account) sees and manages everything across the whole organization — every department's employees and campaigns, the audit log, other admin accounts, and the organization's logo/settings. A department admin is scoped to their own department only: their own department's employees, campaigns they create, and their own raised support tickets. They can still view (but not manage) an org-wide campaign that touches their department. Only a core admin can create department-admin accounts or view the full audit log.

Risk = a behavioural likelihood score (click/download/submit/report rates from an employee's own history) multiplied by a department impact weight (e.g. Finance/IT/Executive carry more weight, since a compromised account there matters more). Compliance findings are a transparent, editable rules table (services/compliance_mapper.py) that cites exact GDPR articles and Sri Lanka PDPA sections with their quoted text — never a black-box score. See each finding's citation box on a campaign or employee page for the exact source text and organizational impact/penalty exposure.
